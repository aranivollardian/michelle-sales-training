Session 1

The BBQ Challenge: Positioning Yourself

Before you can sell anything, you need to know what you are selling and who you are selling it to. This session is about finding the words that make people lean in and ask you more.

The BBQ Challenge

Imagine you are at a backyard barbecue on a Saturday afternoon. Someone asks you "So, what do you do for work?" Most technical people answer this question badly. They either go too deep into the technology, or they are too vague. Neither gets the other person interested.

This exercise is the foundation of everything else in this programme. If you cannot explain what you do to a stranger at a BBQ in a way that makes them want to know more, you will struggle to do it on a sales call, in a proposal, or on LinkedIn.

The Problem

Most AI professionals describe what they build rather than what they solve. Buyers do not care about your tech stack. They care about their problem and whether you can fix it.

The Formula

1
Who you help

Name the type of person or business. Be specific. "Companies" is too broad. "Mid-size businesses with sales teams" is better.

2
The problem you solve

Describe the pain in plain language. What is the frustrating, time-consuming, or expensive thing they are dealing with?

3
How you solve it (simply)

One sentence on your approach. Use words like "automate", "build", "set up" rather than "deploy a multi-agent RAG pipeline".

4
The outcome

What changes for them? Saved time, reduced cost, better decisions, fewer errors. Make it tangible.

Example

"I help businesses automate their most tedious manual work using AI. So if a company has people spending hours every week copying data between spreadsheets, or manually researching leads online, I build tools that do it in minutes. One client saved about 20 hours a week on lead research alone."

Notice: no jargon, no mention of specific models or APIs, clear outcome, and it naturally invites a follow-up question.

Positioning Your AI Services

Positioning is the strategic decision about who you serve and what you are known for. Without clear positioning, you end up competing on price with every other freelancer who "does AI stuff".

The three questions

  1. Who is your ideal client? Industry, company size, role of the buyer. The more specific, the easier it is to find them.
  2. What is the one problem you solve better than anyone? Pick the thing where you have the strongest proof.
  3. Why should they choose you? What is different about working with you versus hiring an agency or using an off-the-shelf tool?
Key Principle

Position yourself as a specialist, not a generalist. "I build custom AI automation for mid-market e-commerce brands" will always beat "I do AI consulting for businesses". Specialists command higher fees, get more referrals, and close faster.

Pricing: value, not time

One of the biggest mistakes technical freelancers make is pricing by the hour. When you price by the hour, your client is buying your time. When you price by the project or by the outcome, your client is buying a result.

Start by understanding what the problem costs your client. If manual lead research costs them $80,000 a year in wages and you can automate 80% of it, your solution is worth $50,000+ to them, regardless of how long it took you to build.

Where to use your BBQ pitch

Once you have your pitch nailed, use it everywhere: your LinkedIn headline and summary, the opening line of cold outreach emails, your website hero section, the first 30 seconds of any sales call, and the way you introduce yourself in meetings.

Exercise

Write your BBQ pitch

Write 3 different versions. Each no longer than 3 sentences. Test them on someone who is not technical. If they ask a follow-up question, it is working.

Bonus: Try one version that does not mention "AI" at all. Focus purely on the outcome.

Session 2

Pipeline Building, Outreach, and Winning Conversations

Now that you can position yourself, it is time to start conversations. This session covers prospecting, running calls that convert, and handling objections with confidence.

Sales Mindset: The Basics

Calling to sell something can feel intimidating, especially if you have never done it. These principles will help you build confidence one conversation at a time.

"Hi, how are you?"

Something as simple as asking "hi, how are you?" and then actually pausing to hear the answer is powerful. Practise it with everyone: your barista, receptionists, colleagues. The pause is what makes it genuine.

Ask for help

People are wired to want to help. Opening with "I was wondering if you could help me?" shifts the dynamic immediately. You are not pushing a product. You are a human being asking for assistance.

Example Opening

"Hi Susan, it's Michelle. I was wondering if you could help me please?"

They do not know who you are, but because you are asking for help, they will most likely say "yeah, sure." Now you have permission to talk.

Non-verbal communication (even on the phone)

Even on a call, the other person can hear your energy. Smile (you can hear a smile), use your hands while talking, and stand up and walk around if you can.

The 30 to 60 second challenge

Try to talk to the other person for 30 to 60 seconds without saying who you are or why you are calling. Chat about the weather, ask about their day. Build rapport before business.

Give 2 options

Rather than "what works for you?", give two specific options: "Does Thursday at 2pm or Monday at 3pm work?" Clear choices force a decision.

Ask for 15 minutes

Nobody is too busy to help someone for just 15 minutes. Once you are in the meeting, the conversation will naturally extend if there is genuine interest.

The Elevator Pitch

Your elevator pitch needs to sound like you are explaining your work to a mate at a BBQ. Write it up and keep it visible until you have it memorised.

Key Principle

If your pitch sounds like a pitch, it is not good enough yet. Rewrite it until it feels like something you would actually say to a friend.

Cold Calling Framework

Calling is 10% what you say and 90% how you say it. An opening that acknowledges you are interrupting their day shows honesty and empathy.

The opening

Try: "I didn't catch you at a bad time, did I?" People instinctively want to say "no", which gives you permission to continue.

Subtle commands

The word "listen" is a gentle command. Using the prospect's name makes them more attentive: "Listen, the reason I'm calling, Sarah, is that most businesses are finding it incredibly challenging to..."

Waterfall your call

Think of your call as water flowing down a mountain. Plan for key questions that act as "rocks" to direct the conversation. Prepare for decision points, not every sentence.

Exercise

Map your waterfall

Map out a call flow for your service. Start with your intro, identify the 2 to 3 likely responses, and prepare follow-ups for each until you reach a booked meeting or clear next step.

Objection Handling

When someone objects, it is the beginning of a real conversation, not the end.

1
Qualify the objection

Ask questions. Listen carefully. Do not interrupt.

2
Ask "what else?"

Keep asking until they have nothing more. Now you have the complete list.

3
Respond to the objection itself

Address the specific concern. If your solution is not right, be honest.

4
Document it

Write down how you handled it. Build a library of responses over time.

The Rapid Pitch Development Method

This technique fast-tracks your sales skills from weeks down to days.

1
Let go of outcomes

The goal is to build your pitch muscle, not close a deal.

2
Pitch One

Pick one style. Call 10 to 20 people back to back. Record what worked.

3
Pitch Two

Switch to a completely different style. Another 10 to 20 calls. Record data.

4
Repeat with 4 to 5 styles

Do this across a full day.

5
Build your own

Like LEGO, take the best pieces from each and build something uniquely yours.

Getting a Meeting via a Call

1
Use their name immediately

"Hi Wendy, lovely to meet you."

2
Simple, clear introduction

One sentence. No jargon.

3
Communicate the value

Why should they give you 15 minutes?

4
Give choices

"Does Thursday afternoon or Friday morning work better?"

5
Ask, then stop talking

Let the silence work for you.

Be Honest, Be Present

Find the top 3 things you love about what you do and lead with those. If you do not know the answer, say so and follow up later. Listening is the most important skill in sales. If what you are offering is the right solution, the conversation will flow naturally.

Session 3

Nurturing Connections and the Art of the Follow Up

Getting a first meeting is only the beginning. What separates people who "network" from people who actually win work is what happens before, during, and after that meeting. This session covers how to prepare for meetings, how to follow up effectively, and how to nurture connections over time so opportunities come to you.

Before the meeting: do your homework

The single biggest mistake people make is walking into a meeting unprepared. Five to ten minutes of research transforms the quality of the conversation and shows the other person you actually care about their business.

Your research checklist

1
LinkedIn

Check their profile and their company page. Understand their background, how long they have been in the role, and what they have been posting about. Look for mutual connections. Connect with them before or immediately after the meeting.

2
Their company

Google their company. What do they do? How big are they? Have they raised funding, been in the news, or recently launched anything? Understanding the size and stage of the business helps you tailor your conversation.

3
Tech stack (if relevant)

If you are meeting a potential client, check what tools they use. Services like BuiltWith or SimilarWeb can tell you about their CRM, marketing tools, and website traffic. This gives you talking points and helps you understand their technical maturity.

4
Your angle

Before the meeting, think about what you want to learn from this person and what value you can offer them. Have your own highlights and achievements ready to draw on. What is the most relevant example of work you have done that would resonate with their situation?

Key Principle

The goal of a first meeting is almost never to pitch. It is to understand their challenges, share relevant examples, and build enough rapport that they want to keep talking to you. Spend more time asking questions than talking about yourself.

In the meeting: discovery over pitching

The best salespeople spend very little time directly pitching. Instead, they ask great questions that uncover the prospect's real challenges, and then use specific examples to show how they have solved similar problems before.

Discovery questions that work

  • "If I had a magic wand and tomorrow your biggest challenge was solved, what would I solve?" This gets straight to the real pain, not the surface-level answer.
  • "How long have you been here? What has changed the most?" Understanding their story builds rapport and reveals what matters to them.
  • "How does a customer usually find you and buy from you?" Understanding their customer journey helps you identify where AI or automation could add value.
  • "What is the most manual, time-consuming part of your week?" This is gold for someone selling AI services. Let them describe the pain and then connect it to what you do.
The Rule

Be genuinely interested in their business. If you are, good questions flow naturally. If you are just waiting for your turn to pitch, they will feel it.

After the meeting: short-term follow up

What you do in the 24 hours after a meeting determines whether the relationship progresses or stalls. Most people send a generic "nice to meet you" email. You are going to do more.

The follow-up email framework

1
Thank them and reference something specific

Show that you were actually listening. Mention something they said that you found interesting. This is not flattery. It is proof that the meeting was worth their time.

2
Send value

If you discussed something specific in the meeting, follow up with something useful: an introduction you promised, a relevant article, a case study, or a resource. Deliver on anything you committed to immediately.

3
Include a clear next step

Do not end with "let's stay in touch." Instead, suggest a specific next action: "I would love to show you a quick example of how this works. Does next Thursday or Friday afternoon work for a 15 minute call?"

Do Not Rush to Proposal

Sometimes one meeting is not enough to fully understand their needs. If you feel that another meeting would be useful, schedule it. Do not rush to send a proposal just because you feel the pressure to move things along. A well-timed second meeting will produce a much stronger proposal than a rushed first attempt.

After the meeting: medium-term nurturing

Not every meeting turns into an immediate opportunity. Many of the best clients come from connections you nurtured over weeks or months. The key is to stay visible and valuable without being annoying.

Nurturing tactics

  • If they are interstate or hard to reach, select dates you will be in their area and mention it: "I am going to be in Melbourne on the 15th. Would love to catch up in person if you are free." This is much harder to say no to than a generic "let's connect sometime."
  • Share relevant content. If you see an article, a new AI tool, or industry news that is relevant to something you discussed, forward it with a short note. This keeps you front of mind and positions you as someone who is across the space.
  • Make introductions. If you know someone who could be useful to them, make the introduction. This is one of the highest-value things you can do. People remember the person who connected them with the right person at the right time.

Long-term relationship building

The best opportunities come from relationships you have built over time. This is not about hard selling. It is about being consistently present, useful, and easy to think of when someone has a need.

The long game

  • Engage on LinkedIn. Like and comment on their posts. This is low effort, high visibility. It keeps you in their feed and shows you are paying attention.
  • Share your own content. Write about what you are learning, building, or thinking about in AI. This positions you as someone who is active and knowledgeable in the space, and gives people a reason to reach out to you.
  • Always be connecting. Think about your network as an ecosystem. Who would benefit from knowing each other? Making introductions is the fastest way to build goodwill and become the person everyone comes to.
Promoters vs Time-Wasters

Not everyone in your network is equally valuable. Learn to identify your Promoters: people who actively refer you, introduce you to others, and champion your work. Invest time in these relationships. Also learn to spot Time-Wasters: people who take lots of meetings but never commit or refer. Do not write people off too early, but be honest with yourself about where your time is best spent.

Exercise

LinkedIn audit

Go through your LinkedIn connections and identify 10 people you could casually reach out to this week. These should be people in your existing network who either work at companies that could use AI services, or who are well-connected in industries you want to target. For each person, write one sentence on why you are reaching out and what you could offer or ask. The message should feel like a natural catch-up, not a sales pitch.

Session 4

Your CRM: The Foundation of Every Sales Practice

A CRM (Customer Relationship Management system) is the single most important tool in sales. It is where you track every person you speak to, every conversation you have, and every deal you are working on. This session covers what a CRM is, why it matters even for a solo freelancer, and how to set up HubSpot from scratch.

Why is a functioning CRM important?

A CRM is not just a contact list. It is the system that ensures you never lose track of a conversation, never forget a follow-up, and never have to rely on memory to recall what a prospect told you three weeks ago. When used correctly, it does four things:

1
Centralise all contact information in one spot

Track every person you interact with, their company, their role, and any notes about your relationship. No more scattered spreadsheets, sticky notes, or email threads you have to search through.

2
Keep your contacts warm

The sheer number of interactions you manage day to day invites human error. A CRM with tasks and reminders ensures every contact is followed up on a regular basis and nobody falls through the cracks.

3
Track your pipeline

A CRM lets you see all your opportunities in one view. You can see exactly where each deal sits, what needs to happen next, and forecast your income. Without this, you are guessing.

4
De-risk your practice

If everything is in your head, your business is fragile. If it is in a CRM, you have a system that works whether you are having a good week or a bad one. It also means if you ever bring on a collaborator or partner, they can see exactly where things stand.

Why This Matters for You Specifically

As someone from a technical background, you intuitively understand structured data. Think of a CRM as a relational database for your sales activity. Contacts link to Companies. Companies link to Deals. Deals have stages, amounts, and activities logged against them. The discipline of keeping this data clean is what separates people who "do some freelance work on the side" from people who run a real practice.

How a CRM is structured

Every CRM, whether it is HubSpot, Salesforce, or anything else, is built around three core objects. Understanding this structure is essential before you start using one.

ObjectWhat it storesExample
ContactsIndividual people. Their name, email, phone number, job title, and any notes about them.Sarah Chen, Head of Operations at Acme Corp
CompaniesThe organisations those people belong to. Company name, website, industry, size.Acme Corp, acmecorp.com.au, Manufacturing, 200 employees
Deals / OpportunitiesThe potential projects or sales you are working on. Each Deal has a name, value, stage, and expected close date."Acme Corp - Lead Gen Automation", $15,000, Proposal Sent, Close by 30 June

These three objects are connected. A Contact belongs to a Company. A Deal is linked to both a Contact and a Company. When you log a call or send an email, it gets recorded against the Contact and automatically appears on the Deal. This is what gives you full visibility.

Key Concept

Always associate your Contacts to Companies, and your Deals to both Contacts and Companies. If you do not link them properly, emails will not sync to deals, activity will not show up in the right places, and your CRM becomes a mess very quickly.

The four pillars of a healthy CRM

Whether you are a solo freelancer or part of a 50 person sales team, a healthy CRM comes down to four disciplines:

1
Every contact you speak to is in the CRM

This provides a single source of truth. It also gives you context on any previous communication with that person, so you never repeat yourself or forget something important.

2
Every commercial opportunity has a Deal

If someone has expressed interest in working with you, or you have identified them as a potential client, create a Deal. Make sure each Deal has a name, a dollar amount (even if estimated), an accurate close date, and a linked Company and Contact.

3
Move Deals along the pipeline stages

Your pipeline is a visual board showing where each opportunity sits. As conversations progress, move Deals from one stage to the next. This gives you transparency on what your pipeline looks like and helps you forecast.

4
Log all activity and set follow-up tasks

Log every call (including missed and voicemail), every email, every meeting. After each activity, set a follow-up task with a due date. Every open Deal should always have an active task assigned to it. If there is no next step, the deal is dead.

Setting up HubSpot (Free Tier)

We recommend HubSpot's free CRM to start. It is powerful, widely used, and scales well if you grow. Here is how to get set up step by step.

Step 1: Create your account and connect your email

Go to hubspot.com and sign up for the free CRM using your work email. Once you are in, connect your Gmail or Outlook immediately. This is the most important setup step because it means every email you send or receive will automatically be tracked against the right contact in HubSpot.

For Gmail: go to Settings >> Integrations >> Email Integrations and connect your account. Then when you are in Gmail, tick "Log" to have emails logged against all Deals associated with that email address, and tick "Track" to see when people have opened your emails.

Step 2: Add your contacts

If you have connected your email, Contacts are created automatically when you email someone (including personal ones, so be aware of that). If you need to add someone manually, go to Contacts >> Create Contact and fill in the details.

A useful shortcut: if you use a work email (e.g. sarah@acmecorp.com.au), HubSpot will automatically create a Company record for Acme Corp and pull basic information from the web. This avoids you having to create the Company separately and prevents duplicates.

Step 3: Create Deals from Contacts

The fastest way to create a Deal is to start from the Contact. After you create or open a Contact, look at the right-hand side of the page and click "+ Add" in the Deals box. This lets you create a new Deal or associate an existing one. The benefit of this method is that it automatically links the Contact and their Company to the Deal, so everything is connected from the start.

Step 4: Set up your Deal pipeline stages

Your pipeline is the visual board that shows where each opportunity sits. For a solo freelancer selling AI services, we recommend keeping it simple:

StageWhat it means
QualifyingA new potential opportunity. You have identified them but not reached out yet.
ContactedYou have reached out but no meeting booked.
Meeting BookedA meeting date is locked in.
Qualified to BuyMeeting held and they are a real opportunity.
Proposal SentYou have sent a proposal or scope of work.
Closed WonThey have said yes and you are starting work.
Closed LostThey have said no or gone quiet.

You can view your pipeline as a Board (Kanban style, with cards you drag between columns) or as a Table. Try both and see what works for you. The Board view is great for a quick visual overview. The Table view is better for filtering and sorting.

Logging activity: the non-negotiable habit

This is the habit that makes or breaks your CRM. If you do not log activity, your CRM is just a fancy address book.

Activity Rules

Every interaction with a prospect or client must be logged against a Deal. This includes all calls (including missed and voicemail), all emails, all meetings, and communications on other platforms like LinkedIn. Always add notes for context, and always finish by creating a follow-up task.

How to log activity in HubSpot

Inside any Deal, you will see a ribbon with buttons for Note, Email, Call, Log, Task, and Meet. This is where all activity is logged.

ActivityHow to log it
EmailShould happen automatically if your email is connected. If not, click the Emails tab in the Deal and click "Log Email". Link the contact, set the date/time, and describe the email.
CallClick the Calls tab, then "Log Call". Link the contact, select the call outcome (connected, left voicemail, no answer), set the date/time, and leave notes.
MeetingClick the Meetings tab, then "Log Meeting". Link all attendees, set the outcome, specify date/time/duration, and add detailed meeting notes.
TaskClick the Task tab, then "Create Task". Name it clearly, add a due date, set a reminder, and leave detailed notes on what needs to happen.

Standard notes format

Every call or meeting note should follow this structure. Consistency makes it easy to pick up where you left off.

FieldWhat to capture
AttendeesWho attended and who missed the meeting
PrioritiesTop 3 priorities of the person you met with
PitchedTop 3 things you pitched or discussed
Next StepsSpecific actions with dates and owners
Follow Up TaskCreate a task in HubSpot with a due date
Post-Meeting Expectations

Log your notes to HubSpot within 15 minutes of completing the meeting. This ensures the content stays fresh and you plan or complete any action items before moving onto other work. Make sure notes are clear enough that someone else could read them and understand what was discussed. Call out action items and next steps at the bottom, separated from the general discussion notes.

Task management: your daily operating system

Tasks are reminders, to-do lists, and your daily sales operating system. They ensure consistent engagement with prospects and make it impossible to forget a follow-up.

Tips for tasks

  • Every open Deal should have an open task associated with it. No exceptions. If a Deal has no next step, it is either dead or you have forgotten about it.
  • Stagger your task due dates. It is easy but impractical to set all tasks for the same day. Spread them out so you have a manageable daily workload.
  • When completing a task, always add a note about what happened, then create the next follow-up task. The cycle never stops until the Deal is Closed Won or Closed Lost.
  • To view all your tasks, go to Sales >> Tasks. You can filter by Due Today, Overdue, Upcoming, and Completed.

The typical Deal journey

Here is how a Deal typically flows through your pipeline, and what should happen at each stage:

1
Deal created in Qualifying

You have identified a potential opportunity. Create the Deal and set a task to reach out.

2
Outreach

You make contact. If they answer and it is positive, move to the next stage and set a follow-up task based on the conversation. If they do not answer, set a follow-up task for next week and try again.

3
Positive outcome: Meeting booked

Move the Deal to Meeting Booked. Log the call. Create a task for meeting prep.

4
Meeting held

If it is a genuine opportunity, move to Qualified to Buy. Log the meeting notes. Create a follow-up task.

5
Proposal sent

Move to Proposal Sent. Set a follow-up task for 3 to 5 business days to check in.

6
Closed

Move to Closed Won (they said yes) or Closed Lost (they said no). Even on a Closed Lost, set a follow-up task for 30 to 60 days. Timing changes and they may come back.

The Golden Rule

If it is not in the CRM, it did not happen. Log activity immediately after every interaction. The 2 minutes it takes will save you 20 minutes trying to remember what you discussed a week later. And always, always set the next task.

Session 5

Discovery: Business Therapy for Your Prospects

Discovery is the most important skill in selling AI services. Before you can solve anything, you need to understand the problem so deeply that you could explain the client's business back to them better than they can. At Vollardian, we call discovery interviews "business therapy" because that is exactly what they are. You are helping someone articulate their pain, and that process alone builds enormous trust.

The golden rule of discovery

Never solution before you understand. The temptation for technical people is to hear a problem and immediately start thinking about how to build the fix. Resist this. Your job in discovery is to scratch and scratch and scratch at the problem until you truly understand it. Only then do you earn the right to talk about solutions.

The Mistake Technical People Make

A prospect says "we need to automate our reporting." A technical person hears this and immediately starts thinking about dashboards, APIs, and data pipelines. A great salesperson hears this and asks: "Tell me about the reporting process today. Who does it? How long does it take? What decisions does it inform? What happens when it is late?" By the time you have asked five more questions, you often discover the real problem is not reporting at all. It is something else entirely.

Understand how the client makes money

Before you can offer a solution, you need to understand the client's business model. This sounds obvious, but most freelancers skip it. If you do not understand how they make money, you cannot connect your solution to their revenue, and if you cannot do that, you are just selling "a cool AI thing" rather than a business outcome.

The customer journey map

One of the most powerful things you can do in a discovery call is walk the prospect through their own customer journey. Ask them to describe, step by step, how a customer finds them, evaluates them, buys from them, and stays with them. This reveals where the friction is, where the manual work lives, and where AI can make the biggest impact.

1
How do customers find you?

Referrals? Advertising? Events? Inbound from the website? Understanding the top of funnel tells you where the opportunities are.

2
What happens between first contact and buying?

How many touchpoints? Who is involved in the decision? How long does the sales cycle take? Where do deals stall or fall apart?

3
What happens after they buy?

Is there onboarding? Do they need support? How do you keep them as a customer? What makes them leave?

4
Where is the manual work?

At each stage, ask: "What part of this is still done by hand? What takes the most time? What breaks most often?" This is where your AI solutions will live.

Why This Works

When you can articulate a client's customer journey back to them clearly, two things happen. First, they trust you because you have taken the time to understand their world. Second, you can now position your solution in the context of their revenue, not just their processes. "I can automate the lead enrichment step that currently takes your team 15 hours a week" is much more compelling than "I can build you an AI lead enrichment tool."

The art of scratching the problem

Most people ask surface-level questions and accept surface-level answers. Great discovery goes deeper. The technique is simple: keep asking "why" and "what else" until there is nothing left to uncover.

Layered questioning

Start broad, then go deeper with each question. Here is what it looks like in practice:

Example: Layered Discovery

"What is the biggest challenge your team is facing right now?"
"We are struggling with lead quality."

"Tell me more about that. What does poor lead quality look like for you?"
"We spend a lot of time on leads that go nowhere."

"How much time would you estimate? And what does that cost you?"
"Probably 20 hours a week across the team. And our close rate is about 5%."

"What have you tried to fix it?"
"We have tried different lead sources but the quality is inconsistent."

"If you could wave a magic wand and fix this overnight, what would the ideal state look like?"
"We would only spend time on leads that are actually a good fit, and we would know that before the first call."

Now you understand the real problem, the cost of the problem, what they have already tried, and what success looks like. You are now in a position to talk about solutions. Not before.

Questions that unlock the real problem

  • "If I had a magic wand and tomorrow your biggest challenge was solved, what would I solve?"
  • "Walk me through what happens on a typical day/week for your team."
  • "What part of that process frustrates you the most?"
  • "What have you already tried to fix this?"
  • "If this problem stayed exactly as it is for another 12 months, what happens?"
  • "Who else in the business is affected by this?"
  • "How do you measure success today? What does good look like?"
  • "What would need to be true for you to invest in solving this?"
Hot Tip

Do not rush to fill silences. After you ask a deep question, let the prospect sit with it. The best answers come after a pause. If you jump in to rephrase or offer options, you lose the chance to hear what they really think.

Structured discovery interviews

When you are exploring a larger opportunity, or when the prospect has multiple stakeholders, you should run structured discovery interviews. This means speaking to more than one person in the organisation using a consistent set of questions, so you can compare perspectives and identify patterns.

Why structure matters

Different people in the same business will describe the same problem differently. The CEO will talk about revenue impact. The ops manager will talk about time wasted. The analyst will talk about data quality. By asking the same core questions to each person, you get a multi-dimensional view of the problem that no single conversation could give you.

How to run structured interviews

1
Prepare a core question set

Write 8 to 10 questions that you ask every single person. These should cover the problem, its impact, what has been tried, and what success looks like. Keep them open-ended.

2
Run them one on one via video

This is critical. Do not do group interviews. People are far more open and honest when they are alone with you on a video call. In a group setting, people filter their answers based on who else is in the room, especially if their manager is present.

3
Let the conversation breathe

Start with your standard questions, but follow the thread wherever it goes. The structured questions are your backbone, not a script. Some of the best insights come from tangents.

4
Take detailed notes immediately

Log your notes within 15 minutes of the call. Include direct quotes where possible. These will be gold when you write your proposal.

5
Synthesise across interviews

After all interviews, look for patterns. Where do people agree? Where do they disagree? The disagreements are often the most valuable insight because they reveal misalignment that you can help resolve.

Business Therapy

This is why we call it business therapy. You are giving each person a safe space to talk openly about what is broken, what frustrates them, and what they wish was different. Many of them have never been asked these questions before. By the time you finish the interviews, the prospect feels understood, you have deep insight into the real problem, and your proposal practically writes itself.

Discovery for AI services specifically

As someone selling AI solutions, you have an additional layer to your discovery. You are not just understanding the business problem. You also need to understand their data, their technical environment, and their readiness for automation.

AI-specific discovery questions

  • "Where does the data for this process currently live? Spreadsheets? A database? Someone's inbox?"
  • "How clean is the data? Is it structured or is it a mess?"
  • "Have you tried any automation or AI tools before? What happened?"
  • "Who would be using this day to day? How technical are they?"
  • "What does your decision-making process look like for new technology?"
  • "Is there a budget allocated for this, or would we need to build a business case?"
Resist the Urge

Even with these technical questions, your job is still to listen, not to solution. If the prospect says "our data is a mess", do not immediately say "I can build a pipeline to clean it." Instead ask: "Tell me more about that. What kind of mess are we talking about?" You are still scratching. The solutioning comes later.

When to move from discovery to solutioning

You know discovery is complete when you can confidently answer all of these:

  • How does this business make money?
  • What is the specific problem I am solving?
  • What is the cost of that problem (in time, money, or risk)?
  • What have they already tried?
  • What does success look like for them?
  • Who are the decision-makers and what do they care about?
  • Is the data and technical environment ready for what I want to build?

If you cannot answer any of these, you need another conversation before you write a proposal. Do not guess. Ask.

Exercise

Build your discovery question set

Write a standard set of 10 discovery questions tailored to the kind of AI services you offer. Include a mix of business questions (how they make money, what the problem costs them) and technical questions (where the data lives, what they have tried). Practise asking them out loud until they feel natural. Bring this to the next session and we will role-play a discovery call together.

Session 6

Pricing: You Are Almost Certainly Charging Too Little

If there is one universal truth about freelancers and consultants, it is this: almost everyone underprices themselves at the start. This session covers how to think about pricing, how to have the budget conversation, and how to write proposals that connect your price to the value you deliver.

The uncomfortable truth about pricing

Most technical people set their prices based on what feels "fair" or what they would be comfortable paying. This is the wrong starting point. Your price should be based on the value you create for the client, not on how long it takes you or what your salary equivalent would be.

Real Talk

At Vollardian, we once tripled our price overnight. Not a single client left. What that told us was not that we were brave. It told us we had been dramatically undercharging for years. Every dollar we left on the table before that was money we gave away for no reason. Most freelancers are in the same position and do not realise it.

Know your floor: the day rate calculation

Before you can price by value, you need to know your floor. This is the minimum you should ever charge, and it is based on a simple calculation from your current salary.

1
Start with your annual salary

Take your current Nearmap salary as a baseline. Let us say it is $100,000.

2
Divide by working days

There are roughly 230 working days in a year (after public holidays and leave). So $100,000 / 230 = approximately $435 per day.

3
Add 50% margin

As a freelancer, you do not get super, leave, sick pay, equipment, or job security. You also spend time on admin, marketing, and sales that you are not billing for. Adding 50% margin accounts for this. So $435 x 1.5 = approximately $650 per day.

4
This is your floor, not your price

$650 per day is the absolute minimum you should consider. It is not what you charge. It is the number below which you are literally better off staying employed. Your actual price should be significantly higher, based on the value you deliver.

Why Day Rates Are a Trap

Knowing your day rate is important for your own internal maths, but you should almost never quote a day rate to a client. When you quote a day rate, the client starts counting your days. They start thinking about how many hours you worked. They start comparing you to other people's day rates. You have turned yourself into a commodity. Instead, quote fixed prices based on the outcome you are delivering.

Fixed price, value-driven pricing

This is how you should price your work. A fixed price for a defined outcome. The client knows exactly what they are paying and exactly what they are getting. You are not selling hours. You are selling a result.

How to set a fixed price

  1. Estimate the effort internally using your day rate (e.g. "this will take me about 10 days, so my cost is $6,500").
  2. Then ask: what is this outcome worth to the client? If you are automating a process that saves them $80,000 a year in wages, your solution is worth a significant fraction of that. $15,000 to $25,000 would be reasonable and still a massive ROI for them.
  3. Price based on the value, not the effort. If the gap between your cost ($6,500) and the value to the client ($80,000 saved per year) is large, you should be capturing more of that gap.
The Value Pricing Formula

Your price should sit somewhere between your cost (day rate x estimated days) and the value to the client (what the problem costs them). The closer you can get to the value end, the better. If you are pricing at your cost, you are leaving money on the table. If you are pricing above the value, you will not win the work.

The budget conversation

This is the part that makes most people uncomfortable. You need to ask about budget. Not asking about budget is like building a house without knowing if the client has $200,000 or $2,000,000. You will either massively over-scope or under-scope the work.

How to ask

Be direct but casual. Something like: "Before I put together a proposal, it would be really helpful to understand the budget you are working with for this. Do you have a range in mind?" Most of the time, people will give you a number or at least a rough indication.

When they say "I don't have a budget" or "I don't know"

This happens often, and it is rarely true. They almost always have a number in their head. They just do not want to say it first. Here is how to handle it:

The Range Technique

Offer them ranges to react to. "For a project like this, we typically see three levels of investment. At the lighter end, around $5,000 to $8,000, we would focus on [X]. At the mid range, around $12,000 to $18,000, we would include [Y]. And for a comprehensive build, it is usually $25,000 to $35,000 which covers [Z]. Which of those feels closest to where you are thinking?"

This does two things. First, it anchors the conversation around professional pricing rather than whatever low number they might have been imagining. Second, it gives them permission to pick a range without feeling like they are committing to a specific number. Their reaction tells you everything you need to know about where to pitch your proposal.

Common pricing mistakes

1
Pricing based on what you would pay

You are not the buyer. Your sense of what is "expensive" is irrelevant. The client is buying a business outcome, not a personal purchase. A $20,000 project that saves $100,000 per year is cheap.

2
Discounting before anyone asks

Never pre-emptively lower your price. If the client has not pushed back on the number, you are solving a problem that does not exist. Quote your price. Be quiet. Let them respond.

3
Charging by the hour

The better you get, the faster you work. Hourly pricing punishes efficiency. If you build something in 2 days that would take someone else 2 weeks, you should earn more, not less.

4
Offering too many options

One clear recommendation is better than five options that confuse the client. If you offer tiers, keep it to two or three at most, and always recommend one.

5
Forgetting scope creep

If you do not define what is included and what is not, you will end up doing twice the work for the same fee. Your proposal must clearly state what is in scope and what would be additional. Learned this one the hard way.

Writing the proposal

A good proposal is not a document about you. It is a document about the client's problem and how you are going to solve it. The structure should follow the arc of your discovery conversations.

Proposal structure

1
How we understood your business

This is NOT a copy of their website. It is your detailed understanding of their needs based on what they told you in discovery. When the client reads this section and thinks "yes, they get it", you have already won half the battle.

2
The problem and its cost

Articulate the problem clearly and quantify it where possible. "Your team spends approximately 20 hours per week on manual lead research, which at an average loaded cost of $50/hour represents roughly $52,000 per year in labour alone."

3
How we can help

Your recommended solution, described in terms of outcomes, not technical specifications. The client does not care about your tech stack. They care about what changes for them.

4
Detailed activity and timeline

Break the work into phases or milestones. This shows the client you have thought it through and gives them confidence you can deliver.

5
Investment

State the price clearly. Do not bury it. Do not apologise for it. If you have done good discovery and the proposal reflects their real needs, the price should feel justified. Use the word "investment", not "cost".

6
Scope and exclusions

Be explicit about what is included and what is not. This protects you from scope creep and sets clear expectations from day one.

Never Send a Proposal Without a Meeting

Try not to send a proposal without having a meeting scheduled to walk through it. If you just email it, you lose the ability to present the thinking behind your recommendations, handle objections in real time, and gauge their reaction. Frame it as: "I would love to walk you through the proposal. Can we set up 30 minutes this week?"

Payment terms and milestones

For any project over a few thousand dollars, break the payment into milestones. This protects both you and the client.

A typical structure

  • 50% upfront on signing (this confirms commitment and covers your initial costs)
  • 25% at a defined midpoint milestone (e.g. when the MVP is delivered or the first phase is complete)
  • 25% on final delivery and sign-off

Never do all the work before receiving any payment. And always, always have a signed scope of work before you start. Verbal agreements are not enough. This is one of those things that feels awkward to insist on but will save you from real pain later.

A Lesson from Experience

If you take one thing from this session, let it be this: always have a signed scope of work with payment milestones before you start any work. The absence of a signed agreement creates ambiguity, and ambiguity always works against the person doing the work. It does not matter how good the relationship is. Get it in writing.

Session 7

The Commercial Mindset

Everything up to this point has been about skills and processes. This session is about how you think. A commercial mindset is what separates someone who delivers good technical work from someone who becomes indispensable to their clients. It is the difference between being a pair of hands and being a trusted adviser.

Become mates with your client

The best client relationships are the ones where you can pick up the phone, share an idea, or ask for help without it feeling like a formal interaction. This does not happen overnight. It happens through consistent, genuine interest in the other person and their business.

Get to know them as people. What drives them? What are they worried about? What are they excited about outside of work? When you build this kind of rapport, two things happen: they start calling you with ideas and opportunities (rather than you always chasing them), and they become far more open with you about the real problems in the business, not just the sanitised version.

The Goal

You want to get to a place where the client sees you as an extension of their team, not an external contractor. When they are thinking about a new challenge or opportunity, your name should be one of the first that comes to mind. That only happens if the relationship goes beyond transactional.

The Week 1 wow factor

First impressions set the tone for the entire engagement. Before you start any project, ask yourself: "How am I going to wow this client in the first week?"

This does not mean delivering the entire project in five days. It means showing the client something they did not expect. A quick insight from their data that reveals something they had not noticed. A one-page summary of their competitive landscape. A prototype or mockup that brings the vision to life before you have even started building. A small, tangible win that makes them think: "This person gets it."

Why This Matters

The wow factor in Week 1 does two critical things. First, it builds immediate confidence that they made the right decision hiring you. Buyer's remorse is real, especially with freelancers. Second, it creates momentum. A client who is excited in Week 1 is easier to work with for the entire engagement. They are more responsive, more collaborative, and more likely to refer you.

Be curious: understand how the business makes money

This was covered in the discovery session, but it deserves repeating because it is the absolute core of a commercial mindset. If you do not understand how the business makes money, you cannot connect your work to their revenue, and if you cannot do that, everything you build is a nice-to-have rather than a must-have.

Go deeper than the surface

Understanding how a business makes money is not the same as knowing what they sell. You need to understand the entire customer journey: how customers find them, what convinces them to buy, what the onboarding looks like, what makes them stay, and what makes them leave. If you are going to change or improve any part of the sales process, you need to understand all of it first.

The Test

After your first few meetings with a client, you should be able to draw their customer journey on a whiteboard from memory. If you cannot, you do not understand the business well enough yet. Keep asking questions.

Connecting the dots

A commercial mindset means you are always connecting information. Who works where? Who reports to whom? What are their individual motivations? What pressures are they under? What did they mention in passing three weeks ago that suddenly becomes relevant now?

This is not about being manipulative. It is about being useful. When you connect the dots faster than anyone else, you become the person who sees opportunities and risks before they are obvious. You can make introductions that nobody else would think of. You can flag a problem before it becomes a crisis. You can suggest an idea at exactly the right moment because you have been paying attention.

Adapt constantly

The information you gather in Week 1 will be different from Week 4. Priorities shift. People change roles. New competitors emerge. A commercially minded person is always updating their mental map of the client's world. They do not assume that what was true last month is still true today.

Listening and building memory

Here is a counterintuitive technique: consider leaving your notebook and laptop closed in meetings. Instead of frantically taking notes, actually listen. Be fully present. Make eye contact. React to what people are saying.

Then, immediately after the meeting, write your notes from memory. You will be surprised at how much you retain when you are truly paying attention rather than splitting your focus between listening and typing. The notes you write from memory also tend to capture the important things rather than a word-for-word transcript that buries the key insights in a wall of text.

The Benefit

When you are fully present in a meeting, the client feels it. They feel heard. They share more. And the things you remember after the meeting are usually the things that actually matter, not the filler. This is how you build a deep, intuitive understanding of the client's business over time.

Speed and urgency

In consulting and freelancing, speed is a competitive advantage. Responding quickly. Delivering quickly. Making decisions quickly. Clients notice when you reply to an email within the hour instead of within the day. They notice when you deliver a draft two days early. They notice when you come to a meeting with ideas you clearly thought about overnight.

This does not mean rushing or cutting corners. It means being biased toward action. If you can do something now, do it now. Do not wait for the perfect moment or the perfect version. A good deliverable today is almost always better than a perfect one next week.

Client momentum

Every client engagement has momentum, and it is your job to understand it and manage it. Momentum is not a fixed thing. It changes constantly, and if you are not paying attention to it, it will slip away before you realise.

Reading the momentum

1
Early engagement (the diagnostic phase)

Momentum is highest at the start. The client is excited, they are investing time, and they want to see results. This is where your Week 1 wow factor matters most. Move fast, show value early, and keep the energy high.

2
Mid engagement (the build phase)

This is where momentum often dips. The initial excitement fades and the hard work begins. Watch for signs: are weekly check-ins getting stale? Are they still engaged or just going through the motions? Are they responsive or are replies getting slower? If you sense momentum dropping, change something. Bring a new insight. Show an early result. Ask a provocative question.

3
Late engagement (delivery and handover)

Momentum can spike again here if you manage it well. This is where you demonstrate the full value of what you have built. Make the handover feel like a launch, not an ending. Set up the client for success after you leave.

The Warning Signs

If weekly check-ins have become routine and nobody is bringing new ideas, momentum is dying. If the client stops responding to emails within the day, momentum is dying. If you find yourself doing work without clear direction or feedback, momentum is dying. The fix is almost always the same: bring something new to the table. An insight, a question, a challenge, an idea. Anything that re-engages the client and reminds them why they hired you.

Exercise

Your commercial mindset checklist

For any current or future engagement, ask yourself these questions weekly. Could I draw this client's customer journey from memory? What is one thing I could do this week to wow them? Is the momentum where it should be, or is it slipping? Who in my network could I introduce them to? What have I learned this week that changes how I think about their business? Make this a habit and your client relationships will transform.

Session 8

Scoping, Delivering, and Managing Expectations

You have won the work. Now you need to deliver it without scope creeping, burning out, or losing the client's trust. This session covers how to write a clear scope of work, how to manage a project from kick-off to handover, and how to handle the inevitable moments when things do not go to plan.

The scope of work is your contract

A scope of work (SOW) is not a formality. It is the single document that protects both you and the client. It defines what you are delivering, when you are delivering it, and what is not included. Without one, every conversation becomes a negotiation about what was "agreed".

What a good SOW contains

1
Project description and objectives

A clear, plain-language summary of what you are building and why. This should mirror the language from your discovery conversations.

2
Deliverables

A specific list of what the client will receive. "An AI-powered lead enrichment tool deployed on AWS" is a deliverable. "Help with leads" is not.

3
Timeline and milestones

Break the work into phases with dates. Each milestone should have a clear definition of what "done" looks like.

4
What is out of scope

Explicitly state what is not included. This is where most disputes happen. If the client might reasonably expect you to do something that you are not planning to do, call it out here.

5
Payment terms

Amount, milestone-based payment schedule, payment terms (e.g. Net 14), and what happens if additional work is needed (your variation rate).

6
Client responsibilities

What do you need from them? Access to data, a point of contact, timely feedback, API credentials. If the project stalls because they do not provide something, the SOW should make it clear that was their responsibility.

Non-Negotiable

Never start work without a signed SOW. It does not matter how good the relationship is or how keen the client seems. Verbal agreements and unsigned documents create ambiguity, and ambiguity always works against the person doing the work. Get it signed. Then start.

Managing scope creep

Scope creep is when the client asks for things that were not in the original agreement. It happens on every project. The question is not whether it will happen, but how you handle it.

The three responses to scope creep

  • "Yes, we can do that. It is within scope." If it genuinely falls within what was agreed, just do it. Do not make a fuss.
  • "Yes, we can do that. It would be a variation." If it is new work, say so clearly and give them a price. "That is a great idea. It was not part of the original scope, so I would estimate it at an additional $X. Want me to put together a quick scope for it?"
  • "That is a great idea. Let us park it for Phase 2." If the timing is wrong but the idea is good, acknowledge it and move it to a future phase. This shows you are listening without letting it derail the current project.

Running the engagement

A well-run engagement has rhythm. The client always knows what is happening, what is coming next, and what you need from them.

Weekly check-ins

Set up a regular weekly meeting (15 to 30 minutes). Use it to share progress, flag blockers, and align on priorities for the coming week. Keep it structured: what was done, what is next, what you need from them. If these meetings start feeling stale, change the format. Bring a demo, a new insight, or a provocative question.

Communication principles

  • Over-communicate rather than under-communicate. The client should never have to wonder what is happening.
  • Flag problems early. If something is going to be late or is not working as expected, tell them immediately. Do not wait until it is a crisis. Clients can handle bad news. They cannot handle surprises.
  • Manage expectations relentlessly. Under-promise, over-deliver. If you think it will take 5 days, say 7. Then deliver in 5.

Handling difficult moments

Not everything will go smoothly. Code breaks. Data is messier than expected. The client changes direction mid-project. APIs go down. The skill is not in avoiding these moments but in how you respond to them.

The Framework

When something goes wrong: (1) Own it immediately. Do not deflect or make excuses. (2) Explain what happened in plain language. (3) Present your plan to fix it, with a revised timeline. (4) Execute the fix. Clients respect people who handle problems with transparency and speed far more than people who never have problems (because those people are usually just hiding them).

Exercise

Write your SOW template

Create a template scope of work that you can adapt for each new project. Include all six sections above, with placeholder text for the parts that change per client. Having a template means you never start from scratch and you never forget an important clause. Bring it to the next session for review.

Session 9

Building a Repeatable Sales Engine

This final session is about turning everything you have learned into a system that runs consistently, not just when you remember to do it. The goal is to build a freelance practice that generates opportunities reliably, so you are never dependent on a single client or a lucky referral.

From project work to a practice

There is a difference between doing freelance projects and running a practice. A freelance project is reactive: someone asks you to build something, you build it, you get paid, you wait for the next one. A practice is proactive: you have a pipeline, a positioning, a pricing model, a delivery process, and a referral engine. Everything you have covered in this programme is a building block toward that.

Your weekly sales rhythm

Even when you are busy with client work, you need to maintain a minimum level of sales activity. If you only sell when you are quiet, you will always have feast-and-famine cycles. Here is a simple weekly rhythm:

M
Monday: Pipeline review

Open your HubSpot. Check overdue tasks. Review where each Deal sits. Set your priorities for outreach this week. 15 minutes.

W
Wednesday: Outreach

Send 3 to 5 messages. Follow up on open conversations. Share a piece of content on LinkedIn. Reach out to a dormant contact. 30 minutes.

F
Friday: Reflect and nurture

What happened this week? Log any outstanding activity in the CRM. Send a "thinking of you" message to a connection. Engage with 5 LinkedIn posts from people in your network. 15 minutes.

The Minimum Viable Rhythm

One hour per week. That is all it takes to maintain a pipeline when you are busy. The mistake most freelancers make is going all-in on sales when they are quiet, then dropping it completely when they are booked. An hour a week, every week, prevents the feast-and-famine cycle.

Referral strategies

The best leads come from referrals. Someone who has been recommended to you is already warm. They trust you before you have even spoken. Building a referral engine is about making it easy for people to recommend you.

How to generate referrals

  • Do great work. This is the foundation. If the client is genuinely impressed, they will talk about you without being asked.
  • Ask directly. After a successful project, say: "I am really glad this worked out well. Do you know anyone else who might benefit from something similar?" Most people will not refer you unless you ask.
  • Make introductions first. The fastest way to get referrals is to give them. When you connect people in your network, they naturally want to reciprocate.
  • Stay visible. Post on LinkedIn. Share case studies (with permission). Speak at events. The more visible you are, the easier it is for people to remember you when someone asks "do you know anyone who does AI?"

Productising your services

As you do more projects, you will notice patterns. The same types of clients have the same types of problems. When you see this, you have an opportunity to productise: turn a custom project into a repeatable offering with a clear scope and a fixed price.

The benefits of productising

  • Easier to sell (the client knows exactly what they are getting)
  • Easier to price (you know your costs because you have done it before)
  • Easier to deliver (you have a proven process)
  • Easier to scale (you can eventually bring on help because the process is documented)
Example

Instead of "I build custom AI solutions", you could offer "AI Lead Enrichment Audit: a 2-week engagement where I analyse your existing lead data, build a custom AI enrichment pipeline, and deliver a scored and enriched dataset. Fixed price: $8,000." This is specific, tangible, and easy for a client to say yes to.

Your personal brand

Your personal brand is what people say about you when you are not in the room. For a solo freelancer, it is your most valuable asset. Everything you have built in this programme contributes to it: your BBQ pitch, your LinkedIn presence, the quality of your work, the way you follow up, the way you handle problems.

Building your brand as an AI professional

  • Write about what you are learning and building. Short LinkedIn posts about real problems you have solved. Not thought leadership fluff, but genuine insights from the trenches.
  • Be known for something specific. "The person who automates manual processes for mid-market companies" is a brand. "AI consultant" is not.
  • Deliver consistently. Your brand is built one interaction at a time. Every email, every meeting, every deliverable either strengthens it or weakens it.
